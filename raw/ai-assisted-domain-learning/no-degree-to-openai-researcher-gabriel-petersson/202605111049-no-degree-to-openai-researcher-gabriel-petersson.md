## Dropping Out and the First Startup
Time: 00:00 - 05:05

Host: Gabriel, you posted a tweet: "Five years ago I dropped out of high school in Sweden to join a startup with close to zero experience. Today I'm joining OpenAI as a research scientist to build AGI with Sora." How did you get there?

Gabriel: I'd always been thinking about AI since reading *Superintelligence* and *Life 3.0* — both by Swedish authors. But I always thought I was too dumb. Then my cousin called and said he'd met someone with a product recommendation idea, they needed to start selling immediately. I was supposed to go to a party that night. He said no. I took the next bus to Stockholm and never went back to high school.

Host: So what was the startup?

Gabriel: We built a product recommendation system for e-commerce stores. None of us knew anything about startups. Cold emails didn't work. Cold calls — I was this random 18-year-old, no credibility. My best move was showing up in person with A3 printouts: their old recommendations on the left, our new ones on the right, a hundred copies in a folder. I also had a console script ready to go live in the first meeting, plus an A/B test built in. I could close everything in one visit.

## Learning to Code Through Real Problems
Time: 05:05 - 09:09

Gabriel: I started with a Udemy Python course, made a dumb duck-dodging game. Tried Andrew Ng's ML course. Thought I was too dumb for it. But when I actually had to build recommendations, scraping integrations, and A/B tests at the startup, everything clicked. Real problems make learning simple — you know exactly what you need next. You go step by step, Stack Overflow, ask friends. The pressure of a real job is everything.

Host: What advice would you give another high school dropout?

Gabriel: I was extremely lucky. I grew up in Vargön, middle of nowhere in Sweden, knew no engineers. I met a programmer once in high school and was starstruck. When you're not surrounded by people building things, it all seems impossible. The key is getting into the market fast, solving real problems, having accountability. Now with ChatGPT you don't even need prior knowledge — you just need to show you're high-agency and can extract information from AI.

## Top-Down Learning vs. Bottom-Up: AI Changes Everything
Time: 09:09 - 13:13

Gabriel: The fastest way to learn is top-down: start with a real problem, recursively dig into what you need to solve it. Bottom-up — math → linear algebra → intro ML → four years before touching anything real — is extremely inefficient. It was the only scalable model before because top-down requires a teacher always available. Now ChatGPT is that teacher, on demand. I can barely take universities seriously that don't teach ChatGPT as part of their curriculum.

Host: And this shifts how education works?

Gabriel: Universities no longer have a monopoly on foundational knowledge. You start with a problem, ask GPT what project to do, write it, debug it, then ask "why does this module make the model learn?" — it explains matrix multiplication, you ask about the math intuition, you go all the way down. You get foundational knowledge without the bottom-up path.

## Schools Are Getting AI Completely Wrong
Time: 13:13 - 14:14

Gabriel: The perception of AI in schools is broken. Students think: "something to cheat with." Teachers think: "something to ban." That becomes a reinforcing loop. Neither group builds intuition for learning *from* AI. I'm happy when my friends in Swedish universities say "I give GPT old exam questions and ask it to find the fundamental concepts they share, or generate ten new ones." But teachers are still heavily anti-AI, which makes no sense. The real loss is that no one teaches AI as a learning tool.

## How Gabriel Taught Himself ML at Sora with ChatGPT
Time: 14:14 - 20:20

Gabriel: At Sora I needed to learn video and image models from scratch. I asked GPT: "What are the most fundamental concepts of image and video models?" It said autoencoders and diffusion models. I said: "Write all the code for a diffusion model." Got a wall of code I didn't understand. Then I debugged it with GPT, built intuition piece by piece — "what does this block do, why does this residual connection help the model learn, how do gradients flow differently because of it?" I kept drilling until I really understood.

Host: So you recursively fill gaps until it clicks?

Gabriel: Exactly. The skill is knowing when you have a gap and knowing what it feels like when something truly clicks. One trick: "explain this like I'm 12." GPT connects AI concepts to real-world analogies — embeddings as books in a bookstore — which makes it easy to reason about. You chase aha moments as frequently as possible. That's your utility function.

Host: You explain it back to the model to verify, almost like Feynman's technique?

Gabriel: Yes. Query until you understand, then explain your understanding back and ask if it's correct. GPT clarifies the gaps you missed. Recursive gap-filling.

## Developing the Reflex to Always Query AI
Time: 20:20 - 24:24

Gabriel: Build the habit: the moment you have a question, get it into ChatGPT. This took me a year to internalize. My cousin was pushing me from day one. Now I always have tabs open. I write code, throw it in: "is this good, any bugs, how can I improve this?" I ask a hundred questions a day. You just constantly learn.

Gabriel: Prompt hacks matter too. I tell it: "be extremely direct and concrete, always show intermediate states and tensor shapes, make sure I have intuitive understanding of *why*, and if you're unsure show me options with what others tried and why it worked or didn't." You're optimizing the path to the aha moment.

Host: That's a hundred thoughtful questions a day. You can outpace 99.9% of people in the world as a high school dropout.

Gabriel: And I'd add: there's still huge value in working with the best humans — models are trained on all internet data, which includes mixed opinions. But AI gets you 95% of the way there. Combine both: work with exceptional people AND constantly query AI.

## AI Enables Non-Traditional Paths to Elite Research Roles
Time: 23:23 - 28:26

Gabriel: I'm doing a job that traditionally requires a PhD. Five years ago, if you told someone a top AI lab would hire someone with no ML or math background, they'd say impossible. But I can do the job just by using ChatGPT. This will double-digit increase world GDP. Anyone can go do bio research, hardware, anything — you just start. It's twenty dollars a month.

Host: How do you use ChatGPT to push the frontier of video models at Sora day to day?

Gabriel: You look at the video output, see something wrong, tweak the architecture or data, train, stare at videos, note what improved. Loop. Where AI helps: "Here's my entire codebase, give me ten ideas to improve this specific problem." It generates ideas, references papers. For papers: I don't read them word for word. I ask: "Compared to the previous technique, list exactly what this paper does differently, be concrete." If it's interesting, I paste my code and the paper in and ask GPT to implement it. But I always read through the resulting code thoroughly. I'm not a vibe coder.

## Building a Career Without a Degree: Contracting and Feedback Hunting
Time: 28:26 - 37:39

Gabriel: After my first startup I always had my sights on San Francisco. Pre-ChatGPT, the best thing I could do was work with the very best people. I interviewed my interviewers: "Do you do code reviews? Will you really comment on my PRs so I know my mistakes?" I took mostly contract roles to stay mobile. The biggest mistake people make early in their career is staying at the same company too long.

Host: You relentlessly sought feedback?

Gabriel: Hunt feedback aggressively. Tell someone: "I loved your review, can we go through every comment on a call?" People are shocked — no one ever says they *like* feedback. Call them. You learn enormous amounts from first-principles explanations of *why* code is written a certain way.

Gabriel: To get to the US, I joined Dataland (a scalable Airtable) with an engineer who left a hundred comments per PR and loved teaching. Then I went to San Francisco on an ESTA visa, networked, and joined MidJourney. After MidJourney I realized I might qualify for an O1 visa. One thing we used: my Stack Overflow posts, which have millions of views — peer-reviewed, helps people, counts as academic publication criteria.

## Getting Hired Without a Degree: Show Value, Skip Recruiters
Time: 37:39 - 42:42

Gabriel: The number one thing: build a demo that communicates your value within three seconds. You don't need complex code — you need people to immediately understand you're a good engineer. Companies just want to make money. Show them how you help them make money. Remove every piece of career advice you've ever received and start from that truth.

Gabriel: Degrees only matter because nobody shows they can actually do the work. Also: avoid recruiters. They have no incentives to find the best person — they have incentives to not make a mistake. So they default to proxy signals like school names. Talk directly to technical people. Every startup wants high-agency people who can learn. Offer to work for free for a week. A hundred percent will say yes.

Host: Even if you go to college, you can apply to jobs while enrolled. Once you have one real job, no one cares about the degree.

Gabriel: Exactly. For hyper-ambitious people: drop out as fast as possible. But you can do the safe version — stay enrolled, apply to jobs in parallel. College is a great experience — you meet interesting people, you even learn things — just inefficiently.

## Advice to the 18-Year-Old Who Doesn't Know What to Do
Time: 43:42 - 47:46

Gabriel: My suggestion: make a leap of faith into software engineering. It's the best field for this because you can show all your work with links. Make games. Build a simple demo. Have a story about why you're high-agency and can learn fast. Maybe screenshot an example of you learning something quickly with ChatGPT. Hiring managers will love it. Send that to five hundred people. One will say "I'll give you a shot." You'll make ten dollars an hour at first but you're in real context, real problems, real economic pressure. From there: keep rolling.

Gabriel: Marketing is another path. At 16, cold emailing "I can do marketing" gets ignored. But if you go into their website, redo one piece of their marketing for free, and email them the result — they'll respond. You already did part of the job. The bar for "considered work" is incredibly low.

## Academia's Gatekeeping and the Ego Behind It
Time: 47:46 - 54:51

Gabriel: I'm deliberately provocative on Twitter about academia — not because academics have done bad work; they've produced incredible research. The problem is one specific belief: that foundational knowledge can only be learned bottom-up, and you need years of prerequisites. This belief actively gatekeeps others. A 17-year-old asks "can I learn ML?" and gets: "You need calculus, linear algebra, four years, then you can touch real ML." That's wrong.

Host: You posted: "It takes three days to learn diffusion models top-down. Six years before you can learn it bottom-up."

Gabriel: The emotional reason people defend bottom-up so hard: if you spent ten years on it and a high school dropout learned it in three days with ChatGPT, that's threatening. Their ego gets hurt. When someone's career is built on telling students university is essential for foundational knowledge, and then that becomes unnecessary, they'll do everything in their power to maintain that narrative. But universities no longer have a monopoly on foundational knowledge.

## Most Advice Is Useless — Learn to Discard It
Time: 54:51 - 01:01:57

Gabriel: People who give you career advice are almost always misaligned with your interests. Someone who spent five years in college will tell you to do the same — with good intentions — but they've never compared it to alternatives. Their opinion is locked in by the sunk cost of their own choices. Your opinions nearly always come after your incentives. I generally recommend people to discard most advice.

Host: Were your parents supportive when you dropped out?

Gabriel: My parents were at the extreme low-ego end of parenting. They never pushed grades beyond "just don't fail." I was actually angry — why won't you push me harder? But it turned out to be a gift. High-ego parents attach their dreams to their children. Mine just supported whatever I did.

Gabriel: 70% of people are in permanent light suffering because they are allergic to making mentally tough decisions when there's an option to do nothing. Your brain doesn't alert you when you're avoiding something hard. If you're unhappy at a job, your brain convinces you you're learning the maximum you possibly can — even if you've been there three years. Force action: get introduced to a company, start interviews, let the momentum carry you through the emotionally hard parts.

## Why San Francisco — and How to Get There
Time: 01:06:00 - 01:10:04

Gabriel: Simple reasons first: talent density is much higher, salary is dramatically higher. If you're hyper-ambitious, moving to San Francisco is objectively correct — you have no other real options if you're going all-in on startups. Capital flows freely, network effects are extraordinary. First week here, people always say "I had no idea there were this many people who cared about what I'm working on." That's real motivation — not motivational speeches.

Host: So how does someone from Sweden actually move here?

Gabriel: Step one: become highly skilled. Get into real companies, solve real problems. Step two: find a company willing to sponsor you. You need to be excellent because sponsorship is overhead. But great software engineers are globally scarce — there are never enough. That's your leverage. Prove you provide massive value remotely and people in San Francisco are very happy to sponsor you. Stack Overflow, GitHub, open-source contributions all count toward O1 visa criteria in creative ways.

## Closing: Anyone From Anywhere Can Do This
Time: 01:10:04 - 01:14:13

Host: You're basically giving advice to your younger self — someone from the middle of nowhere who thought they weren't smart enough.

Gabriel: I could have been here four years earlier if someone had told me what I'm trying to tell people now. People listening to this are probably already in the top one percent — the fact that you're spending an hour looking for how to do something meaningful already puts you there. Top one percent maps to the top 200–500 startups in the world. Just connecting those dots should make it feel real.

Gabriel: The biggest blocker for a lot of people is thinking they're not smart enough. I thought I was an idiot growing up. But it's incredibly easy to underestimate how much you can do. Remove the belief that there's a gatekeeping credential standing between you and the work. Show what you can do. Start today.
