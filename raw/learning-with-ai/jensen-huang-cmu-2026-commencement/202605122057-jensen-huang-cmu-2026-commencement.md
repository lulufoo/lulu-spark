## The American Dream: An Immigrant Family's Journey
Time: 02:36 - 05:04

Jensen Huang: His parents' dream was the American dream. At nine, he and his brother were sent alone to a Baptist boarding school in Onida, Kentucky — coal country, a few hundred people. Two years later his parents arrived with almost nothing: his father a chemical engineer, his mother a school maid who woke him at 4 a.m. to deliver newspapers; his brother got him a Denny's dishwashing job, "which at the time felt like a major career advancement."

His takeaway on America: *"Not easy, but full of opportunities. Not a guarantee, but a chance."*

At Oregon State he met his wife Lori at 17 — sophomore lab partners, she was 19, an older woman. He beat out 250 classmates to win her heart. Married forty years; two children, both at NVIDIA.

## Founding NVIDIA: Learning Through Failure
Time: 05:04 - 07:44

Jensen Huang: At 30, co-founded NVIDIA with Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem to build a computer that could solve problems ordinary machines couldn't. None of them knew how to run a company. *"I just thought: how hard could it be? It turns out, it is super hard."*

Their first technology failed. They nearly ran out of money. He flew to Japan, admitted to Sega's CEO that the contracted technology wouldn't work, asked to be released from the deal — and then asked to still be paid. *"It was embarrassing, humiliating, and one of the hardest things I have ever done."* Sega's CEO said yes.

Lesson learned: being CEO is about keeping the company alive, not wielding power. Honesty and humility can be met with generosity even in business. With that money they reset, invented new chip-design methods still in use today, and reinvented NVIDIA over and over for 33 years. *"Each failure is just another learning moment, a humility moment, a character-strengthening moment."*

## The AI Revolution: Computing's Complete Reset
Time: 07:44 - 10:20

Jensen Huang: His career began at the PC revolution's dawn; theirs begins at the AI revolution's dawn. CMU is one of AI's true birthplaces — the Logic Theorist in the 1950s, the Robotics Institute in 1979.

Having lived through every platform shift — mainframes, PCs, internet, mobile, cloud — he calls what's coming *"bigger than anything before."* For 60 years the paradigm was unchanged: humans write software, machines execute instructions. That era is over. AI has moved computing from human coding to machine learning, from CPUs to neural-network GPUs, from instruction-following to understanding, reasoning, planning, and tool use. A new industry now manufactures intelligence at scale, and every industry will be transformed.

## Promise and Responsibility: Guide It, Don't Fear It
Time: 10:20 - 12:51

Jensen Huang: AI naturally raises fears — jobs disappearing, people left behind, technology becoming too powerful. Every major revolution has. But when society engages technology openly and responsibly, it expands human potential far more than it diminishes it.

AI is *"the automation of understanding, reasoning, and problem solving"* — among the most powerful technologies ever created. It brings both great promise and real risk. Scientists and engineers must advance capability and safety together. Policymakers must build thoughtful guardrails without blocking progress. *"History shows that societies that retreat from technology do not stop progress — they only surrender the opportunity to shape it."* The answer is not to fear the future but to guide it wisely and ensure its benefits reach as many people as possible.

---

## AI for Everyone: Universal Access and Reindustrializing America
Time: 12:51 - 15:01

Jensen Huang: Today only a fraction of people can write software. AI changes that — a shopkeeper can build a website, a carpenter can design a kitchen, the AI writes the code. *"Everyone is now a programmer."* For the first time, computing's power can close the technology divide.

Like electricity and the internet, AI demands trillions in infrastructure — chip factories, computer factories, data centers, advanced manufacturing facilities. This is the largest technology buildout in human history and a once-in-a-generation opportunity to reindustrialize America. *"Electricians, plumbers, ironworkers, technicians, builders — this is your time."* Enormous energy demands are also driving grid modernization and accelerating sustainable energy investment.

---

## AI Amplifies Human Purpose, Not Replaces It
Time: 15:01 - 16:54

Jensen Huang: Tasks will be automated; some jobs will disappear; many new ones will emerge. Software coding increasingly automated — yet software engineers using AI tackle more ambitious challenges, so demand grows. Radiology scans increasingly automated — yet radiologists using AI diagnose better, so demand grows. *"AI does not replace human purpose. It amplifies human capability."*

His sharper warning: *"AI is not likely to replace you — but someone using AI better than you might."*

Therefore society must do four things at once: advance AI safely, create thoughtful policies, make AI broadly accessible, and encourage everyone to engage. Opportunity should not belong only to those who can code.

## This Moment Is Yours: Run, Don't Walk
Time: 16:54 - 18:41

Jensen Huang: The class of 2026 enters the world at an extraordinary moment — a new industry being born, a new era of discovery beginning. AI will accelerate human knowledge and help solve once-unreachable problems. No generation has entered with more powerful tools or greater opportunity. *"We are all standing at the same starting line. This is your moment to help shape what comes next. So run, don't walk."*

CMU's motto: **My heart is in the work.**

*"Put your heart in the work. Build something worthy of your education, your potential, and the people who believed in you long before the world did. Congratulations, Carnegie Mellon class of 2026."*
