## Opening at Davos: From “What Can AI Do?” to “What Is AI Doing?”
Time: 00:00 - 01:15

Host: Welcome to Journal House—and to everyone online. We're at Davos with Dario Amodei, CEO of Anthropic. Last year the conversation was about AI's potential and capabilities; this year it feels more like what AI is doing to the world. Do you think businesses, policymakers, and governments are doing enough to prepare?

Dario Amodei: No.

## Public Mood Swings vs a Smooth “Moore’s Law for Intelligence”
Time: 01:15 - 04:30

Dario Amodei: I've watched this field for 15 years and worked in it for 10. Public reaction oscillates wildly in two ways. On capabilities, every three to six months the media flips between “it's going to change everything” and “it's all a bubble.” What I see is a smooth exponential—like Moore's law for compute, but for intelligence. Models get more cognitively capable every few months, constantly.

On whether AI is good or bad, polarity swings too: heavy concern about risk and misuse in 2023–24, then “AI opportunity” in 2025, now shifting back. Anthropic's approach has been constancy—both upside and downside are real. I still believe the radical upside I wrote about in *Machines of Loving Grace*—curing cancer, eradicating tropical diseases, spreading development. I'm also writing more now about what can go wrong.

## Excited and Worried: High GDP Growth With High Unemployment
Time: 04:30 - 08:00

Dario Amodei: The signature of this technology may be very high GDP growth together with very high unemployment and inequality—a combination we've almost never seen. Historically, high growth meant lots of jobs. This disruption is different: 5–10% GDP growth alongside ~10% unemployment isn't logically inconsistent; it just hasn't happened that way before. I'm excited and worried for both reasons.

Take AI coding: with Claude Opus 4.5, some engineering leads at Anthropic tell me they no longer write code—they let Opus do it and they edit. We shipped Claude Co-work, a non-coding version of Claude Code, built in about a week and a half almost entirely with Opus. Engineers still have work—for now—but that won't last forever.

Software may become cheap or essentially free; the idea that you must amortize software across millions of users may break down. You might spin up a small app for a meeting for cents. At the same time, whole careers built over decades may disappear. We can adapt—but there's almost no awareness of what's coming or how large it is.

## Measuring the Transition, Helping People Adapt, Sharing Growth
Time: 08:00 - 12:30

Host: In a world of high GDP growth and high unemployment, how might society organize itself? Can you be concrete?

Dario Amodei: Three steps. First, measure: our Anthropic Economic Index—updated several times over the past year—tracks in a privacy-preserving way how Claude is used: tasks, automation vs augmentation, industries, diffusion across U.S. states and countries. Until we can measure the shape of this transition, policy will be blind.

Second, help people adapt—within jobs or across jobs. There may be more work in the physical world and less in knowledge work (robotics is slower). Some roles still value a human touch; the market will test how much. When software and then knowledge work get cheap, corporate moats scramble—we've never really asked what moats mean in that world. Teach people what to expect.

Third, government has a role when displacement is macroeconomically this large. The pie will grow; money will be there; budgets may even balance from growth alone. The issue is distribution. Worry less about disincentivizing growth and more about everyone getting a share—even if that cuts against today's prevailing sentiment. Technology will force our ideas to change.

## Speaking to Policymakers in Public, Not Behind Closed Doors
Time: 12:30 - 14:30

Host: Are you speaking to people in this administration? Anthropic hasn't always been first on the guest list.

Dario Amodei: I've said these things to them directly. We agree on plenty—the administration's mid-year AI action plan had many good ideas; we probably agreed with the vast majority. Mostly we want to say this in public and have a democratic debate. We don't control policy. The most useful thing we can do is describe what we see, provide data, and let the public drive policy.

Host: Will you meet officials here? Have you been to USA House?

Dario Amodei: Not yet to USA House—but I will talk to officials during Davos.

## Enterprise Focus, Safety Tradeoffs, and Competition With China
Time: 14:30 - 20:00

Host: You founded Anthropic because OpenAI wasn't taking safety seriously enough. Have competitive pressures—staying ahead of China, etc.—compromised your safety principles?

Dario Amodei: We took a different route: enterprise over consumer. It's hard to fight your own incentives; better to pick a business model with fewer perverse ones. Consumer AI pushes engagement maximization, slop, ads—we've seen that from others. We sell to businesses; we don't need a billion free users in a death race.

We've still made sacrifices. We run tests others skip—deception, blackmail, sycophancy—and publish concerning behaviors. We've pioneered mechanistic interpretability. Have we been perfect? No. Generally we've done a good job.

On China: that's not really about commercial competition. Our public-benefit mission includes worry that if autocracies lead in AI, it's bad for everyone in this room. The main lever is chips—who gets them. It's not about a people or a country; it's about a form of government. AI may suit autocracy uniquely—surveillance, individualized propaganda, breaking into systems, detecting and suppressing dissent, drone armies at scale. It's scary and we have to stop it. I don't think there's enough targeted focus on not selling chips to autocracies—we don't need to “fight” them; we need to not arm them.

## Claude's “Moment,” Revenue Curve, and Co-work
Time: 20:00 - 25:30

Host: Claude is having a real moment—engineers and regular users are getting “Claude-pilled.” How does the business feel versus a year ago?

Dario Amodei: Business growth has been fast but on the same smooth exponential as the technology. Rough rounded revenue: ~$0 → ~$100M in 2023, ~$100M → ~$1B in 2024, ~$1B → ~$10B in 2025. Twitter oscillates between “Anthropic is changing the world” and “Anthropic is destroyed”—but we watch the curve. It might not continue—but empirically it has.

There are breakout moments on a smooth curve. Claude Code hit an inflection with Opus 4.5—gradual improvement until people notice, like boiling a frog. Second: non-technical people inside and outside Anthropic wrestled with the command line to use Claude Code for agentic tasks—organizing todos, projects, folders, summarizing documents. That's unmet demand. We built a better UI in about two weeks; within a day metrics were roughly 4× anything we'd released. People are catching up to capability because it crossed a threshold and we made interfaces accessible.

Host: How do you personally use agentic AI?

Dario Amodei: A fair amount of my job is writing—essays, company communications. Claude helps with sources and drafting.

## IPO, Capital Intensity, and Competing With Gemini
Time: 25:30 - 28:30

Host: An IPO this year is widely expected. Plans?

Dario Amodei: We don't know for sure. Priority is keeping the revenue curve going, selling models, warning about societal impacts, and delivering good impacts. This industry has very high capital demands; at some point private markets only go so far.

Host: Gemini surged; OpenAI declared “code red.” Do you worry about competing with Google?

Dario Amodei: Being different helps. Google and OpenAI fight in consumer—existential for OpenAI as their whole business, and for Google because search is being disrupted. They're more focused on replacing themselves in consumer than on enterprise. Gemini's consumer progress is impressive; Demis Hassabis leads research at Google—I've known him 15 years and I'm rooting for him.

Host: Anthropic doesn't generate images or video—is that a weakness?

Dario Amodei: For enterprise there's little demand for consumer-style image/video slop. Edge cases like slides—we could contract another model. I don't anticipate needing it. Short-form video has a lot of fake, addictive slop—not a market I'm rushing into.

## Scientists vs Entrepreneurs, Geopolitics, and “AI Sovereignty”
Time: 28:30 - 30:45

Host: You said scientists leading AI labs approach this era differently from tech entrepreneurs. What do you mean?

Dario Amodei: This technology is decades of academic research intersecting with internet-scale infrastructure and cash from large platforms. Some labs are led by scientists—my background, Demis's background. Others by the social-media entrepreneur generation. Scientists have a tradition of responsibility for what they build; entrepreneurs of that era were selected and rewarded differently around consumer manipulation—different attitudes follow.

Host: U.S.–EU tensions are high. Could that affect how you operate?

Dario Amodei: We speak only for ourselves—independent. We disagree or agree on policy when it's about AI. I haven't seen reluctance elsewhere to work with us; we're providing models responsibly.

Host: There's a lot of talk about AI sovereignty this week.

Dario Amodei: I don't know what it means either.

## Reader and Audience Questions: Safety, Education, Inequality
Time: 30:45 - 32:08

Host (reader, Trevor Lumis): What's the single most important technical breakthrough still missing for frontier AI to be reliably safe and controllable?

Dario Amodei: More progress on mechanistic interpretability—looking inside models. Training doesn't guarantee behavior; models can say one thing and do another, like humans. Phenomenological testing isn't ground truth. Like MRI for brains, interpretability may be the only ground truth for making models safe and controllable.

Host (reader, Jim O'Connell): How will AI affect K–12 achievement gaps?

Dario Amodei: Short term: cheating is problematic but relatively manageable with different teaching approaches—we've released Claude for education around that. Harder: what skills should we teach when disruption is broad? I can't confidently tell someone which career to pick. Maybe we should move away from a mercenary view of education back toward shaping character and enriching you as a person—that's a safer foundation.

Host (audience): What responsibility do AI labs have when economies and people are left behind—structurally involving them, slowing down?

Dario Amodei: I worry at many scales—developing vs developed world, and within countries. Startups adopt AI fast; big traditional enterprises move slowly—we see it in our data and in U.S. state diffusion. The nightmare: a “zeroth world” of ~10 million people—~7M in Silicon Valley, ~3M scattered—forming a decoupled economy where 10% national GDP growth feels like 50% inside that bubble. That would be dystopian.

Anthropic is working on public health in the developing world, Rwanda's Ministry of Education, the Gates Foundation—catch-up growth should be even faster there, as I wrote in *Machines of Loving Grace*. Within countries we need growth to reach places like Mississippi, not just a contained Silicon Valley—we've done work on economic mobility. Both will need government; ideology won't survive the technology. What sounds politically coded today will become bipartisan necessity—mark my words, in a year or two everyone will think this.

Host: Thank you, Dario—that was fascinating.

Dario Amodei: Thank you for having me.
